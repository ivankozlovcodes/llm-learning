The TOPS Trap

Jetson Nanos, Pi AI HATs, and every $250 box sold as an AI computer. The number on the label measures a resource your workload never runs out of.

~15 minutes Prerequisites: 1, 3 Mission · Glossary · Cheat sheet

A Jetson Orin Nano Super is $249 for 67 TOPS. A DGX Spark is $4,699. If TOPS were the thing that mattered you could buy eighteen Nanos and beat it. This lesson is about why that arithmetic is a trap, and it is the cleanest possible test of whether Lesson 1 actually landed.

The one idea

TOPS measures compute. Decode never runs out of compute. On a Jetson Orin Nano, single-stream token generation uses roughly 0.3% of the advertised 40 TOPS — the other 99.7% is idle, waiting for memory.

Edge AI hardware is marketed on the one specification that is least relevant to running a language model.

The cleanest proof: an accessory that cannot do it at all

Raspberry Pi sells three AI HATs. Their own documentation is unusually blunt about what the first two can do:

Doubling the TOPS from 13 to 26 changed nothing — both are vision accelerators that cannot run a language model at any size. What unlocked generative AI was adding 8 GB of onboard memory. The capability boundary tracked memory, not compute, exactly as Lessons 1 and 3 predict.

Why the compute goes unused: the roofline

A machine can never exceed min(peak compute, bandwidth × operational intensity). The bend between those two limits is the ridge point, and it sits at:

ridge = peak compute ÷ memory bandwidth the operational intensity you must supply before compute becomes the limit

For an original Jetson Orin Nano — 40 TOPS, 68 GB/s — that is 40 × 10¹² ÷ 68 × 10⁹ = 588 FLOPs per byte. Decode supplies about 2. You are short by a factor of nearly three hundred, and no amount of tuning closes a gap of that shape.

This calculation and the measured benchmarks below are from Eric X. Liu, Why Your Jetson Orin Nano's 40 TOPS Goes Unused, which arrives at the same 588 figure independently.

Switch the machine and watch the ridge move. The M1 Max sits at 52 — a balanced design, which is why its prefill-to-decode ratio in Lesson 2 was only 9:1. The Jetson AGX Thor sits at 7,582. Every one of these devices is sold on the number that lives to the right of its ridge, and every agent workload lives to the left.

Predict before you look

The Orin Nano Super raised bandwidth to 102 GB/s and compute to 67 TOPS, for $249. Llama 3.1 8B at Q4_K_M is 4.9 GB of weights. Ceiling, and realistic?

102 ÷ 4.9 = 20.8 tok/s ceiling, so ~13–14 tok/s realistic. Respectable for $249 and about 25 W.

Now notice what did the work: bandwidth went up 50% and so did the token rate. Compute went up 68% and contributed essentially nothing to decode. NVIDIA marketed the "Super" update as a generative AI performance boost, and it was — but through the 102 GB/s, not the 67 TOPS.

Auditing a vendor claim with the rule

Hailo advertises the 10H running Llama2-7B at up to 10 tokens/sec under 5 W. Run it backwards: a 7B model at INT4 is roughly 3.5 GB, so 10 tok/s requires ~35 GB/s of achieved bandwidth. The chip carries LPDDR4X-4267, which on a 64-bit bus tops out around 34 GB/s.

So the claim sits exactly at the theoretical ceiling — meaning either it is measured under ideal conditions you will not reproduce, or the model is smaller than stated. Either way the diagnosis is the same: the part is bandwidth-limited and the 40 TOPS is decorative. You can now do this to any spec sheet in about fifteen seconds.

Caveat: Hailo does not publish the bus width, so the 34 GB/s is inferred from the memory type. Treat it as an estimate — but the conclusion holds across any plausible bus width, which is what makes the technique useful.

The 8 GB wall

Even taking the Orin Nano Super's ~13 tok/s at face value, Lesson 3 kills it for your workload. Of 8 GB, about 6.5 GB is usable. Llama 8B Q4 takes 4.9 GB, leaving 1.6 GB for the KV cache. At 128 KB per token that buys:

1.6 GB ÷ 128 KB per token = ~12,000 tokens of context before the OS takes its share

Your Lesson 2 agent session reached 42,000. A CLI coding agent's system prompt, tool definitions and a couple of file reads will exhaust 12k before the first interesting turn. Quantizing the KV cache to Q8 doubles it to ~24k, which is still not enough.

And the $1,000 used M1 Max still has more bandwidth than any of them.

Where micro PCs genuinely win

None of this makes them bad hardware — it makes them badly matched to your workload. They are excellent at:

  • Vision. CNNs have high operational intensity and small weights. This is what the Hailo parts were designed for and they are superb at it, at a few watts.
  • Small always-on models. A 0.5–3B model for wake-word handling, classification, routing or embeddings, running 24/7 at 5–25 W instead of a laptop at 90 W.
  • Batched throughput at the edge. Many concurrent small requests raise operational intensity and move you toward the ridge — the one case where the TOPS starts to count.
  • Robotics and sensor fusion, which is what the Jetson line is actually built for and where its compute is fully used.

An embedding or classification sidecar for your pi harness on a $249 Nano is a genuinely good idea. Running the agent's main model on it is not.

Check yourself

Question 1

A device advertises 40 TOPS with 68 GB/s of bandwidth. What share of that compute does single-stream decode actually use?

Under one percent — about 0.34%. The ridge point is 588 FLOPs/byte and decode supplies roughly 2, so you sit almost 300× to the left of where the compute would matter. This is why two devices with identical bandwidth and wildly different TOPS generate tokens at the same speed.
Question 2

The 13 and 26 TOPS Raspberry Pi AI HAT+ boards cannot run language models at all. What is the reason?

No onboard memory. Both use the Pi's system RAM across a narrow link and have only small on-chip buffers, which suits vision models with small weights. The AI HAT+ 2 unlocked LLMs by adding 8 GB of its own — a memory change, not a compute one.
Question 3

Orin Nano Super: ~6.5 GB usable, Llama 8B Q4 is 4.9 GB, KV cache is 128 KB per token. About how much context fits?

~12k tokens. 6.5 − 4.9 = 1.6 GB of headroom, divided by 128 KB per token. The model advertises a 128k context window and the hardware can hold a tenth of it — a gap the spec sheet will never mention, and one you can only see by doing Lesson 3's arithmetic.
Question 4

A mini PC is marketed for AI. Which specification should you look up first?

Bandwidth, then capacity. Bandwidth sets how fast tokens come out; capacity sets whether the model and its cache fit at all. Vendors lead with TOPS precisely because it is the largest and least binding number they have. If bandwidth is not on the spec sheet, infer it from the memory type and bus width — or from a measured token rate, as we did with the Hailo.

Where this leaves the buy decision

The micro-PC tier is out for hosting your agent's main model — not because the boxes are slow, but because 8 GB cannot hold a model and an agent-scale KV cache simultaneously. The tier that survives starts at 64 GB, and inside it the ranking is by bandwidth: M1 Max 400, then a three-way tie at 273 between the Mac mini M4 Pro, the Thor, and the Spark.

The genuinely new candidate this lesson surfaces is the Mac mini M4 Pro — the Spark's bandwidth and comparable usable memory, from $1,599, with the caveat that it has no CUDA and Apple charges steeply for the 64 GB configuration. Worth pricing properly when we make the call.
