Lesson 19 · Local LLM Fluency

Embeddings and Retrieval

Lesson 16 told you to use retrieval instead of fine-tuning. Lesson 18 named note-taking as the one tactic you had not built. Both need an embedding model — a second resident model with a cost shape unlike anything else in this workspace.

~22 minutes Prerequisites: Lesson 17, Lesson 14 Mission · Glossary · Cheat sheet
The one idea

An embedding model is a generation model with the head cut off. You run prefill and then stop — pool the hidden states into one vector and discard everything else.

So embedding is pure prefill: no decode, no sampling, and no session KV cache to carry. On your M1 that means it runs at the 118 tok/s end of Lesson 17's asymmetry rather than the 14 tok/s end. It is the one local workload that is unambiguously on the cheap side.

What you get back

One vector per input, L2-normalized, with similarity measured as a dot product. For WeMM-Embedding-2B that vector is 2,048 numbers; for the 9B it is 4,096. "Similar" means pointing in a similar direction — nothing more mystical than that.

The models you named accept text, images, video and visual documents, and return the same kind of vector for all of them, in one shared space. That is what "universal multimodal embedding" means: a screenshot and the sentence describing it land near each other. "WeMM-Embedding-2B is a universal multimodal embedding model built on Qwen3.5. It accepts text, images, videos, visual documents, and interleaved multimodal inputs, and returns a 2,048-dimensional L2-normalized embedding. Audio input is not supported." — tencent/WeMM-Embedding-2B model card.

The verdict on the two you picked

The 9B is not an option on the 8 GB machine. At 6.252 GB multimodal it is 2.05 GB over your entire budget — before any generation model is loaded beside it.

And the score gap does not argue for it anyway: 3.5 points of MMEB-v3 for 3.6× the weights. The 4B is the one worth your attention — it fits, and it takes you 63% of the way from the 2B to the 9B for twice the size. Start on the 2B; move to the 4B if retrieval quality actually disappoints you.

Two things the vision tower teaches you. First, it is nearly size-independent: 0.671 GB on the 2B and 0.624 GB on the 9B. So it is 43% of the 2B's weight and 11% of the 9B's — a fixed tax that hurts the small model far more. Second, it is a separate file. If you only ever embed text, do not load it, and the 2B drops to 1.560 GB. Lesson 15 warned that people budget for mmproj by accident; here it is the difference between comfortable and tight.

Matryoshka: the free 8× win

These models are trained so that the first N components of the vector are themselves a usable embedding. Truncate and renormalize, and you have a smaller vector that still works. The config lists the valid rungs:

matryoshka_dimensions 64 · 128 · 256 · 512 · 1024 · 2048 2B — the 4B ends at 2560, the 9B at 4096

And the payoff, stated by the model card: "On MMEB-v2, 256-dimensional embeddings retain 98.7% of the full-dimensional image and video performance." Eight times smaller, for 1.3%. There is almost no other lever in this workspace with that shape.

The cost of building it

Predict first

The calculator says a 5 MB corpus takes about an hour to index on the M1 with the 2B. Push the slider to 100 MB — roughly a large monorepo's source. Before you do: does that become an afternoon, or something else? And what is the fix?

About 20 hours. Indexing is linear in tokens and the M1's prefill rate is what it is — Lesson 17's asymmetry helps you here, but a fixed 118 tok/s on a 7B does not become fast by being on the cheap side of a ratio. This is a genuinely slow job on this machine.

The fix falls out of having two machines. The index is portable; the model is not. Build on the 64 GB machine, where prefill is several times faster and the 4B or 9B is affordable, then copy the result to the laptop. A 100 MB corpus at 256-dim int8 comes to 57,445 chunks and a 15 MB file — you could email it to yourself, and the travel machine then only needs to embed queries, which are one short string each and effectively instant.

That asymmetry — expensive to build once, cheap to query forever — is what makes retrieval work on hardware that could never fine-tune anything.

The trap: can you actually use the multimodal half?

Here is where a model card stops being enough. These are community GGUF conversions of a model whose own documentation shows vLLM and SGLang, and never mentions llama.cpp. Before building anything on them, three questions had to be answered from source.

QuestionAnswer, verified
Does llama.cpp know this architecture? Yes. LLM_ARCH_QWEN35 maps to the string "qwen35" in llama-arch.cpp, which is what the GGUF metadata key qwen35.pooling_type targets.
Is pooling_type=3 the right thing? Yes. LLAMA_POOLING_TYPE_LAST = 3 in llama.h — last-token pooling, which is what the 2B repo says it injected.
Can you embed an image? Through the server, yes. Through the CLI, no. See below.

llama-embedding, the obvious tool, contains no reference to mmproj or mtmd at all — it is text-only, and will silently give you text embeddings from a multimodal model. llama-server's /embeddings endpoint does pass its multimodal context through to the tokenizer, and accepts a non-OpenAI object form:

{"input": {"prompt_string": "<__media__>", "multimodal_data": ["<base64 of the image>"]}}

The number of <__media__> markers must match the number of items in multimodal_data, and if the server was started without --mmproj you get "Multimodal data provided, but model does not support multimodal requests." Verified in tokenize_input_subprompt(), which the /embeddings handler calls with the mtmd context; marker rules from the server README, which also advises checking /models for the multimodal capability first.

Read the repos, not just the model

TuTuCSF/WeMM-Embedding-9B-GGUF has a README containing nothing but licence frontmatter — no usage, no conversion notes, no statement of what was tested — against 722 downloads. The 2B repo is better: it documents the pooling-type injection and the separate vision tower. But the rest of its README is copy-pasted from upstream, so its Python examples load tencent/WeMM-Embedding-2B — the original weights, not the GGUF you downloaded. Run those examples and you will fetch several gigabytes of the wrong thing and never notice.

This is Lesson 14 in miniature: the quantizer's repo and the model's repo are different claims, by different people, with different evidence behind them.

Hands on, within 4.2 GB

text only — 1.56 GB resident llama-server -m WeMM-Embedding-2B-Q4_K_M.gguf \
  --embeddings --pooling last --embd-normalize 2 -c 4096

with images — 2.23 GB resident llama-server -m WeMM-Embedding-2B-Q4_K_M.gguf \
  --mmproj mmproj-WeMM-Embedding-2B-BF16.gguf \
  --embeddings --pooling last --embd-normalize 2 -c 4096

--pooling last matches what the repo baked into the metadata, and passing it explicitly means you are not trusting a metadata key you did not write. --embd-normalize 2 is Euclidean, which is what "L2-normalized" on the model card means — so cosine similarity becomes a plain dot product. Flag names and the --embd-normalize semantics (-1=none, 0=max-abs int16, 1=taxicab, 2=euclidean) read from common/arg.cpp.

Then truncate to 256 dimensions and renormalize — truncation breaks the unit length, and skipping the renormalize is the single most common way to get quietly wrong similarities.

Where this lands in the mission

Lesson 16 said weights are a bad database and to use retrieval instead. Lesson 18 said structured note-taking — persisting state outside the window — was the one tactic with nothing built for it. This is the missing piece for both.

A pi plugin that indexes your repo and registers a search_code tool converts permanent context cost into on-demand context cost. Instead of reading three files into the window and paying for them on every subsequent turn (Lesson 17's ratchet), the agent retrieves three chunks when it needs them. On a machine where the attention ceiling is 16,000 tokens, that is the difference between a session that lasts and one that does not.

Check yourself

Question 1

Why is embedding cheaper per token than generation on the same machine?

It is prefill and nothing else. You run the forward pass, pool the hidden states, and stop — there is no autoregressive loop, so none of the per-token weight passes that make decode bandwidth-bound. Size helps too, but the regime is the reason.
Question 2

Why does the 9B fail on your travel machine while the 4B does not?

Its weights alone exceed the budget. 5.628 GB plus a 0.624 GB vision tower is 6.252 GB against 4.2 GB of usable memory — over by 2.05 GB, before any generation model. The 4B lands at 3.433 GB and fits, with 0.77 GB to spare.
Question 3

You truncate 2048-dim vectors to 256. What must you also do?

Renormalize. Cutting components off a unit vector leaves it shorter than unit length, so dot products stop being cosine similarities. Re-encoding is unnecessary — that is the entire point of Matryoshka training, and 256 dimensions retain 98.7% of full performance.
Question 4

You run llama-embedding with a multimodal model and no mmproj. Result?

Perfectly good text embeddings, silently. llama-embedding has no multimodal support at all, so nothing complains — you simply never get the capability you chose the model for. Image embedding needs llama-server --mmproj and the multimodal_data object form.
Question 5

Indexing 100 MB on the M1 takes about 20 hours. Best response?

Build it on the other machine and carry the file. The index is portable and small — 100 MB of corpus at 256-dim int8 is about 15 MB. Fewer dimensions shrinks storage but not encoding time, since truncation happens after the forward pass. Bigger chunks reduce the chunk count but not the token count, which is what the time depends on.

Addendum · the 48 GB M4 Added 2026-09-05 — the lesson above stands as written; this is what a third machine changes.

The verdict above — that the 9B is not an option — was a verdict about the 8 GB machine. On the 48 GB M4 all three sizes fit, and the interesting question changes from "which one" to "build or carry".

Better than that: the embedder and a generation model fit at the same time. Ornith-1.5-35B-A3B (21.71) plus the 9B embedder with its vision tower (6.252) is 27.96 GB, leaving about 8 GB — roughly 100,000 tokens of KV cache. A retrieval-augmented agent loop with both models resident, on a laptop, on a plane. On the 8 GB machine neither of those two fits alone.

But keep carrying the index. Indexing is prefill-bound and linear in tokens; the M4 is perhaps 4–8× faster than the M1, which turns a 20-hour job into a 3–5 hour one. That is better and still not something to start on a plane. The reveal above stands: build at home, carry the file, and use the M4's headroom for querying — and for embedding the new material you create on the trip, which is a small incremental job rather than a full rebuild.
