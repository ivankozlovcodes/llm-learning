Lesson 16 · Local LLM Fluency

Fine-Tuning and Soup

Fifteen lessons have treated the weights as fixed. They are not. Changing them is a memory problem you already know how to price — and averaging two changed models together is the cheapest win in the whole field.

~22 minutes Prerequisite: Lesson 5, Lesson 8 Mission · Glossary · Cheat sheet
The one idea

A training run holds four things in memory: base weights, gradients, optimizer state, and activations. Full fine-tuning pays for all four. LoRA deletes two of them and quantization shrinks the third — which is why a 1.7B fine-tune that needs 20 GB the obvious way needs about 2.5 GB the sensible way, and fits on your travel laptop.

And once you have trained two adapters, you do not have to choose between them. Averaging weights is free, and it usually beats picking the best one.

First: you probably should not

Fine-tuning is the most over-reached-for tool in local LLM work. Before spending a Saturday on it, check that the thing you want is not already available for nothing:

The honest research position. A controlled comparison found that "in the standard low-rank settings, LoRA substantially underperforms full finetuning" on programming and mathematics — but that "LoRA better maintains the base model's performance on tasks outside the target domain." For teaching a small model new domain knowledge, LoRA is a weak tool. For nudging behaviour without wrecking everything else, that trade is exactly the one you want. Biderman et al., LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less (arXiv 2405.09673)

The four terms

You have priced inference three times now: weights plus KV cache against a budget. Training adds two terms and inflates a third.

peak training memory
base weights + gradients + optimizer state + activations

Gradients are one number per trainable parameter. Optimizer state is two more for AdamW — a running mean and a running variance. So the full training state is roughly 8 bytes per trainable parameter in MLX, where optimizer state is created with mx.zeros_like(parameter) and therefore inherits the parameter's bfloat16; it is 16 bytes under PyTorch mixed precision, which additionally keeps an fp32 master copy. MLX optimizer state dtype read from mlx/optimizers/optimizers.py; the 16-byte figure is the standard mixed-precision Adam accounting from Rajbhandari et al., ZeRO (arXiv 1910.02054).

Activations are the intermediate values from the forward pass that the backward pass needs. Unlike the other three they scale with batch × sequence length × layers, not with parameter count — which is why a fine-tune can fit and then explode when you feed it one long example.

What LoRA actually does

LoRA freezes the base weights and trains a low-rank correction to selected matrices: for a d × k weight, two skinny matrices B (d × r) and A (r × k), whose product is added to the frozen original. Hu et al., LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models (arXiv 2106.09685)

trainable parameters per adapted matrix
r × (d + k)

Work it for Qwen3-1.7B at mlx-lm's defaults — rank 8, on q_proj and v_proj, in the last 16 layers. Hidden size is 2048; q_proj is 2048→2048 and v_proj is 2048→1024, because the model has 16 query heads and only 8 KV heads:

q_proj: 8 × (2048 + 2048) = 32,768
v_proj: 8 × (2048 + 1024) = 24,576
(32,768 + 24,576) × 16 layers = 917,504 parameters

0.918 M trainable out of 1,720.6 M — 0.053%. Multiply by 8 bytes and the entire gradient-and-optimizer bill is 7 MB. Two of the four terms have effectively vanished.

Check your arithmetic against the tool. mlx-lm prints a Trainable parameters: line — 0.918 M, 0.053% — before the first iteration. If your hand calculation and that line disagree, one of you has the wrong layer count. Format string in mlx_lm/tuner/utils.py. Model geometry from Qwen3-1.7B config.json.

What QLoRA does

With gradients and optimizer state gone, the frozen base weights become the floor. QLoRA lowers the floor by quantizing them — the base is stored at 4 bits and dequantized on the fly, while the adapters stay at full precision. The original paper used it to "finetune a 65B parameter model on a single 48GB GPU while preserving full 16-bit finetuning task performance." Dettmers et al., QLoRA (arXiv 2305.14314)

The measured file sizes make the effect concrete. These are real mlx-community safetensors, not estimates:

Sizes from the Hugging Face file API for mlx-community/Qwen3-1.7B-{bf16,8bit,4bit}. Budget of 4.2 GB from Lesson 5. The 4-bit constant of 0.563 bytes/param reproduces Qwen3-0.6B (0.335 GB) and Qwen3-4B (2.263 GB) exactly — it is a constant you can reuse.

Price your own run

The calculator applies the four-term model. It starts on the configuration this lesson recommends for your 8 GB machine. Change one control at a time and watch which term moves.

Predict first

Leave everything else alone and switch base precision from 4-bit to bf16. The base weights grow by 2.47 GB. Does the run still fit in 4.2 GB — and which single other control would you change to rescue it?

No — it lands around 5.0 GB, roughly 0.8 GB over. The activation term is untouched at ~1.14 GB, the trainable state is still 7 MB. All the damage is in one term.

The instinctive fix is a smaller batch, but batch is already 1. The control that rescues it is gradient checkpointing: it drops activations from ~1.14 GB to ~140 MB by storing only layer boundaries and recomputing the rest, at the cost of an extra forward pass. That gets you to ~4.0 GB — a fit, and slower than simply staying at 4-bit. Which is the lesson: on this machine, quantizing the base is the cheapest lever, and you reach for the others afterwards.

Hands on: a tool-calling fine-tune in 4.2 GB

This runs on the 8 GB M1, with a browser open. It is sized so that the interesting part is the arithmetic you just did, not the wait.

1. Install

pip install "mlx-lm[train]"
MLX is Apple-Silicon only, and this is the whole install. mlx-lm LoRA documentation.

2. Build a dataset in the tools format

mlx-lm reads four dataset shapes — chat, tools, completions and text. The tools shape is the one that matters here: it carries the tool schemas alongside the messages, and mlx-lm renders both through the model's own chat template, so you are training on exactly the string the model will see at inference — the point Lesson 7 made about templates, now on the training side.

One example per line in data/train.jsonl, with a data/valid.jsonl alongside:

{"messages":[{"role":"user","content":"what's in src/"},{"role":"assistant","tool_calls":[{"id":"c1","type":"function","function":{"name":"list_dir","arguments":"{\"path\": \"src\"}"}}]}],"tools":[{"type":"function","function":{"name":"list_dir","description":"List a directory","parameters":{"type":"object","properties":{"path":{"type":"string"}},"required":["path"]}}}]}
Format taken verbatim from LORA.md § Data. Note the arguments field is a JSON string, not an object — the convention differs between model families, so check your target model's template.

The natural source is your own pi transcripts: every successful tool_call in a session is a labelled example of the behaviour you want. Two or three hundred lines is enough to move a 1.7B model's tool-selection habits. Keep every example short — the activation term is the one that scales with sequence length, and one 8k example will cost you more than a hundred 400-token ones.

3. Train

mlx_lm.lora \
  --model mlx-community/Qwen3-1.7B-4bit \
  --train \
  --data ./data \
  --batch-size 1 \
  --num-layers 8 \
  --max-seq-length 1024 \
  --mask-prompt \
  --iters 300 \
  --adapter-path ./adapters-a

Every flag is a term in the equation:

FlagDefaultTerm it cuts
--model …-4bitbf16Base weights, 3.44 → 0.97 GB. mlx-lm switches to QLoRA automatically when the model is quantized.
--batch-size 14Activations, 4×
--num-layers 816Activations and trainable state, 2×
--max-seq-length 10242048Activations, 2×
--mask-promptoffNothing — but it stops the loss rewarding the model for predicting your prompts, which is not the skill you want
Defaults from mlx-lm's example lora_config.yaml. The memory levers are exactly those in LORA.md § Memory Issues, in the order that documentation recommends them.
If it goes quiet

Over-budget training does not crash — Lesson 5's failure mode applies unchanged. macOS compresses, then swaps, and your iterations-per-second falls by an order of magnitude while everything still looks fine. Watch memory pressure in Activity Monitor, not the log. If it goes yellow: add --grad-checkpoint, then drop --num-layers to 4, then fall back to mlx-community/Qwen3-0.6B-4bit at 0.335 GB.

4. Test, then use

mlx_lm.lora --model mlx-community/Qwen3-1.7B-4bit \
  --adapter-path ./adapters-a --data ./data --test

mlx_lm.generate --model mlx-community/Qwen3-1.7B-4bit \
  --adapter-path ./adapters-a --prompt "list the files in src"

Test loss is a weak signal for this job. The signal that matters is a held-out set of prompts where you count how often the model picks the right tool — the same personal eval set Lesson 14 argued for. Build it before you train, or you will have no way to know whether the fine-tune helped.

Soup: the free half of the lesson

You now have a training loop. Run it three times with different seeds and learning rates and you get three adapters, one of which validates best. The obvious move is to keep that one.

The better move is to average them. Averaging the weights of models fine-tuned from the same base "often improves accuracy and robustness", and — the part that matters on an 8 GB machine — "unlike a conventional ensemble, we may average many models without incurring any additional inference or memory costs." Wortsman et al., Model soups (arXiv 2203.05482)

Why it works, and when it does not

Fine-tunes that start from one pretrained checkpoint "often appear to lie in a single low error basin". Inside a basin, the straight line between two solutions stays low-loss, so the midpoint is a valid model — often a better one, because averaging cancels the noise each run picked up from its own seed and data order.

Cross the basin and it collapses. You cannot soup two independently pretrained models — Qwen3 and Gemma 3 average to noise. Same base, or no soup.

The recipe: greedy, not uniform

Averaging everything is the naive version, and the paper measured a better one. Sort your runs by held-out validation score, best first; then walk the list, adding each run to the soup only if the soup's score does not fall. "Before running this procedure we sort the models in decreasing order of validation set accuracy, and so the greedy soup can be no worse than the best individual model on the held-out validation set." Greedy souping also "avoid[s] adding in models which may lie in a different basin of the error landscape, which could happen if, for example, models are fine-tuned with high learning rates" — precisely the runs a hyperparameter sweep produces. Both quotations from Wortsman et al. (arXiv 2203.05482), § Method.

greedy soup
sort runs by val score, best first
soup ← {}
for each run: if val(mean(soup ∪ run)) ≥ val(mean(soup)) then soup ← soup ∪ run

Note what this costs you: nothing at inference. Unlike running three models and voting, the soup is one model of exactly the original size. On a machine with 4.2 GB, that distinction is the whole reason soup is available to you and ensembling is not.

The LoRA trap

Here is the part that catches people, and it is pure algebra. A LoRA adapter is stored as two matrices whose product is the weight change. If you average the two adapters' factors separately, you do not get the average of their effects:

what you wanted
½(B₁A₁ + B₂A₂)

what averaging the factors gives you
B₁+B₂⁄2 · A₁+A₂⁄2 = ¼(B₁A₁ + B₁A₂ + B₂A₁ + B₂A₂)

Half your signal, plus two cross terms that are pure noise — A₂ was never trained against B₁. The fix is concatenation: stack the factors side by side, [B₁ B₂] · [A₁ ; A₂], which multiplies out to exactly B₁A₁ + B₂A₂ with no cross terms. This is PEFT's combination_type="cat", and the documentation flags the consequence: "the rank of the resulting adapter is equal to the sum of all adapters ranks". PEFT add_weighted_adapter reference. The alternatives are to fuse each adapter into its own full model first and average those (correct, and what mlx-lm's mlx_lm.fuse sets you up for), or combination_type="svd", which computes the true sum and re-factors it back down to a chosen rank.

The 8 GB route. mlx_lm.fuse writes a full fused model per adapter, and at 4-bit that is under a gigabyte each — so souping by fusing first is affordable here. Average the fused tensors with a twenty-line mlx.core script, or hand the fused directories to mergekit, which uses "lazy loading of tensors for low memory use" and an out-of-core approach that runs on CPU or "as little as 8 GB of VRAM". mergekit README. Invoke as mergekit-yaml config.yml ./out --lazy-unpickle.

Beyond averaging

Soup is the first rung. The same machinery, one idea further on, treats θ_finetuned − θ_base as a task vector you can scale, add, and subtract. The merge methods you will see named in model cards are all variations on handling interference between those vectors:

Methodmergekit nameWhat it adds
Model souplinear"Simple weighted average of model parameters". The baseline.
Task arithmetictask_arithmetic"Linearly combine 'task vectors' (differences from a base)" — so you can scale or negate a behaviour.
TIESties"Task arithmetic + sparsification & sign consensus". Trims tiny changes, then resolves parameters where two runs disagree on direction.
DAREdare_ties"Task arithmetic + random pruning & rescaling". Drops most of the delta and rescales the rest, which reduces interference.
Method names and descriptions quoted from the mergekit README. Underlying papers: task arithmetic (2212.04089), TIES (2306.01708), DARE (2311.03099).

For your case — several runs of the same job on the same base — plain linear with a greedy selection is the right tool, and the fancier methods are for merging different tasks. Reach for TIES when you have a tool-calling adapter and a summarization adapter and want one model that does both.

Back to the mission

A souped 1.7B adapter that reliably picks the right pi tool is a real routing option (Lesson 12): the cheap local model handles tool selection, the 64 GB machine's model handles the thinking. And it is one more reason to keep tool schemas stable — Lesson 10 argued that from the KV-cache side; fine-tuning adds a second argument, since every schema change invalidates your training data too.

Check yourself

Question 1

LoRA's memory saving comes mainly from removing which parts of the budget?

Gradients and optimizer state. Both scale with trainable parameters, and LoRA cuts those by roughly 2,000×. The base weights are still fully resident — LoRA freezes them, it does not remove them — which is why quantizing the base is a separate and additive win.
Question 2

Your fine-tune fits at 512 tokens but not at 4096. Which term grew?

Activations. Three of the four terms depend only on parameter counts and do not move when you change sequence length. Activations scale with batch × sequence × layers, which is why they are also the term gradient checkpointing exists to attack.
Question 3

Why can you not make a soup from Qwen3-1.7B and Gemma 3 4B?

Different loss basins. Soup works because fine-tunes of one checkpoint "lie in a single low error basin", so the line between them stays low-loss. Independently pretrained models share no such geometry and average to noise. The mismatched shapes and vocabularies would stop you first, but the reason it would fail even if they matched is the basin.
Question 4

Averaging two LoRA adapters' A and B matrices separately gives you what?

Half the signal plus two noise terms. (B₁+B₂)(A₁+A₂)/4 expands to ¼(B₁A₁ + B₁A₂ + B₂A₁ + B₂A₂); the mixed products pair factors that never trained together. Concatenating the factors gives B₁A₁ + B₂A₂ exactly, at double the rank.
Question 5

Which want is not a good reason to fine-tune a local model?

Valid JSON. A grammar makes invalid output unreachable during sampling; training only makes it rarer, and rarer is not the same guarantee. Spend fine-tuning on behaviour a grammar cannot express — which tool, which arguments, when to stop.

Addendum · the 48 GB M4 Added 2026-09-05 — the lesson above stands as written; this is what a third machine changes.

Everything above was sized against 4.2 GB, where LoRA was not a preference but the only option. On the 48 GB M4's ~36 GB the four-term budget opens up considerably:

RunPeak8 GB M148 GB M4
Full fine-tune, Qwen3-1.7B19.60 GBno — 4.7× overfits
QLoRA 4B, batch 4, seq 2048~14 GBnofits
QLoRA 1.7B, the run above1.94 GBfitstrivial
Full fine-tune, Qwen3-4B~44 GBnono

Three consequences. Batch size stops being pinned at 1, so `--grad-accumulation-steps` becomes a convenience rather than a necessity and runs finish in a fraction of the wall clock. Full fine-tuning of a small model is now on the table — which matters because Biderman et al. found LoRA underperforms full fine-tuning precisely on code and mathematics, the domains you care about. And soup becomes practical in one sitting: three runs of a 1.7B QLoRA at batch 4 take an evening rather than a weekend, and greedy souping needs several runs to have anything to be greedy about.
